Why Rural Students Need Help

Madagascar is ranked the 9th poorest nation in the world, with an average per capita GDP of less than $1,000. For rural students, the barriers to education are immense.

⚠

Deeper Poverty

Rural students are poorer than their counterparts in cities. Most families simply cannot afford school fees, supplies, or living costs away from home.

⛪

No Local High Schools

Small villages generally do not have high schools. Students seeking a secondary education must move to a larger town — an expense most families can't bear.

✎

Underfunded Schools

Countryside schools receive less funding and have lower standards of education. Students must often subsidize their own teachers' salaries just to attend class.

♀

Extra Barriers for Girls

Female students must bear domestic responsibilities on top of their schoolwork, making it far more difficult to devote time to education.

How We Help

We facilitate the high school education of students who would not otherwise have the opportunity to study at the high school level.

  • Merit-based selection: We choose students based on their level of need and past academic performance.
  • Full commitment: Each student, along with a parent or guardian, commits to completing high school. We commit to providing a living stipend plus school fees and supplies.
  • Academic standards: Students must maintain passing grades to remain in the program.
  • Empowering girls: At least 50% of our students are female — because educating girls transforms entire communities.
  • Tutoring support: We provide tutoring to our students, with noticeable improvements in performance since the program began.

Our Story

MAD Opportunities was founded by people who saw firsthand the gap between potential and opportunity in rural Madagascar.

What began as a small effort has grown into a program supporting 60 students across 12 high schools in the Ambalavao district, working with the NGO Ny Tanintsika/Feedback Madagascar. Our students attend Youth Camp, receive training in agroecology and life skills, and participate in savings programs — building independence that lasts far beyond graduation.
